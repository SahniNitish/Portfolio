[curious] Immigration is usually sold as a simple meritocracy. Move to a rich country. Study hard. Get a good job. Fit in.
[serious] But look at it through game theory, and a different picture appears. Immigration isn't a neutral elevator. It's a game, and the rules were written by the people already in charge.
[curious] Start with a puzzle. Students from East Asian education systems top the world's PISA rankings. East Asian Americans have some of the highest median incomes in the United States, and fill engineering, medicine and tech.
[serious] Yet research on corporate leadership keeps finding the same thing: they are heavily underrepresented in the executive suite. People call it the bamboo ceiling.
[serious] Studies of some large dating platforms have found a similar penalty, with East Asian men receiving fewer replies than other groups.
[mysterious] The lesson of game theory: doing everything right, by the rules, does not mean you control the system.
[curious] Think of a casino. Bright lights, free drinks, the feeling of a fair chance. But if the games were truly fair, the house would go broke. The house sets the rules so the average player loses, and the casino keeps the money.
[serious] A country that opens its doors to global talent plays a similar role. You are invited to play, but the game is built around the home team's norms, communication style and social networks.
[serious] Follow the rules. Pay taxes. Obey the law. Work hard. Stay quiet. You become a reliable contributor. But contributors are not automatically the people who write the rules.
[mysterious] Play strictly by the rules, and you can trade your skills for a paycheck while staying outside the rooms where status is decided.
[curious] Now zoom out. Game theory says groups with strong trust and cooperation often win repeated games. Networks, mentors, favours and loyalty are how status actually moves.
[serious] That is the real trade-off. Dissolve into the crowd, and you may lose your network. Stay tightly inside your own community, and you may stay outside everyone else's.
[curious] Population matters too, but the numbers are less dramatic than people think. Fertility has fallen below the replacement level of about two point one children per woman across much of the rich world. And in most countries, immigrant fertility drifts toward the local average within a generation or two.
[serious] Forecasts for twenty fifty are guesses built on assumptions about migration and birth rates. Game theory can explain incentives. It cannot predict who ends up running a country.
[curious] Migration is not a modern invention, and it was rarely gentle. Ancient DNA shows early farmers spreading across Europe, and later steppe herders arriving and reshaping the population. Sometimes through conquest, sometimes through mixing.
[curious] Modern, open immigration is recent. It grew out of European expansion, and then, after World War Two, American-led globalization, built to fill labour shortages and power industrial growth.
[serious] Systems like that tend to last only while they serve the people who designed them.
[curious] For developing countries there is a price: brain drain. Doctors, engineers and founders are told success means leaving, to look for money and respect in a richer country's cities.
[serious] And some who go find that comfort comes with a glass ceiling. A big salary under someone else's rules is not the same as power.
[mysterious] Real power comes from building, shaping and sustaining institutions of your own.
[serious] So here is the sobering takeaway of the game theory of immigration: whoever sets the rules tends to collect the spoils.
[curious] For individuals and for nations, the winning move is not climbing a rigged ladder forever. It is knowing when to build a game of your own.
