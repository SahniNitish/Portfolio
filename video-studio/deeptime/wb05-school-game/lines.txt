[curious] School has three simple jobs.
[curious] Teach us to read. Teach us to think. And teach us to keep learning for the rest of our lives.
[serious] So why do schools often produce memorization instead of thinking... competition instead of collaboration...
[serious] And students who celebrate the end of exams by never wanting to open a book again?
[mysterious] What if school isn't broken? What if it's working exactly as the system rewards it to work?
[curious] To understand that, let's look at the man who built the school everyone claims to want... Got students into Yale, Wharton, and Cornell... And then got fired.
[curious] In 2008, after graduating from Yale, an English major joined Shenzhen Middle School to build an international program for students hoping to attend foreign universities.
[curious] What he found looked impressive on paper. But inside the classroom?
[serious] Fifty students sat passively in massive classes. No discussion. No debate. No real exchange of ideas.
[serious] SAT preparation meant memorizing enormous vocabulary lists, not becoming better readers or writers.
[curious] And extracurriculars? Almost everyone joined Model UN. Because looking involved mattered more than being involved.
[curious] So the new teacher changed the game.
[curious] He replaced giant lectures with small discussion seminars. He built a five thousand book English library.
[curious] He helped launch China's first high-school student-run coffee house, where students learned management, finance, and teamwork.
[curious] And he launched a daily student newspaper, reported, edited, printed, and distributed by students every morning. Real reading. Real writing. Real responsibility.
[curious] The result? Record college admissions. The students won. The program worked.
[curious] So everyone celebrated, right? Nope.
[serious] Parents disliked him. Teachers resisted him. Administrators turned against him. He was fired, and blocked from running similar programs again.
[mysterious] His mistake wasn't educational. It was strategic. He changed the classes... without changing the game.
[curious] Game theory says outcomes come from players, their incentives, and their power.
[curious] And a useful starting assumption is this: Players seek the biggest perceived reward for the least possible effort.
[serious] That doesn't mean everyone is selfish or lazy. It means we can't design a system around what people say they want. We have to watch what they reward.
[curious] Parents say they want independent, successful children. But they may also want prestige, high grades, control, and impressive marketing, like foreign teachers standing at the school gate.
[curious] Teachers may care deeply about students. But teaching is still a job. Extra seminars, newspapers, and projects can mean more work for the same salary.
[curious] Administrators may want innovation. But their survival often depends on keeping powerful parents and staff happy. So bold reform becomes... don't cause trouble.
[curious] Government leaders may talk about creativity and innovation. But they also prioritize stability, compliance, and avoiding disruption.
[curious] And colleges? They may genuinely want curious, passionate students. But they are also institutions with budgets, and many need families who can pay full tuition.
[curious] Finally... students say they want top universities. But they also want popularity, comfort, approval, and, when possible, an easy A.
[serious] Again: not everyone. Not always. But enough players, often enough, to shape the system.
[curious] And these players do not have equal power. Parents often hold enormous power because they fund the system.
[curious] Teachers control what happens every day inside classrooms. Administrators negotiate between them, and try to survive.
[serious] Students may be the supposed purpose of school... but often have the least power over how it operates.
[mysterious] So what happens when all these incentives collide? The system reaches a point of convergence. Not the school everyone dreams about. The school everyone can tolerate.
[curious] Parents get impressive grades. Teachers avoid impossible workloads. Administrators avoid conflict. Students avoid painful struggle.
[serious] And real learning? Real learning gets a lovely mention in the brochure.
[serious] That's how you end up with grade inflation, cosmetic innovation, cheating, high teacher turnover, and schools that look successful while quietly failing at their mission.
[serious] Not necessarily because the people are evil. But because bad incentives can make decent people build bad systems.
[curious] But incentives don't appear from nowhere. They sit inside a wider society. The lecture identifies three forces that help determine whether schools improve or decline.
[curious] Cohesion: Do we believe every child's future matters, or only our own child's ranking?
[curious] Openness: Can we admit mistakes and adapt, or do we punish anyone who exposes a problem?
[curious] Energy: Are people motivated to build excellent institutions, or merely protect what they already have?
[curious] When all three are strong, schools can thrive. Think of Finland, or China in the 1980s, when education was widely seen as a path out of poverty. Resources were limited, but respect for teachers and student drive were powerful.
[serious] As wealth and inequality grow, however, education can become a zero-sum contest. Your child's success becomes my child's failure.
[serious] Administrators stop taking risks. Wealthy families depend less on academic effort to secure their children's futures.
[serious] And school stops being a shared project. It becomes a marketplace for advantage.
[curious] So why did the Shenzhen reformer fail? Because he tried to impose a better game on players who hadn't agreed to play it.
[serious] Strict fairness threatened hidden privileges. Harder learning created more work. New responsibilities disrupted established hierarchies.
[serious] And record admissions couldn't save him from the stakeholders whose support he needed.
[curious] Sustainable reform doesn't begin with a heroic outsider rewriting every rule. It begins inside the overlap between existing interests.
[curious] Give parents visible evidence of real growth. Give teachers reforms they can actually sustain.
[curious] Give administrators wins that don't threaten their survival. Give students meaningful challenges, and reasons to value them beyond a grade.
[curious] Then move the equilibrium, one step at a time.
[curious] So the next time you ask, why doesn't school create curious, independent thinkers? Ask a better question.
[mysterious] Who gets rewarded when students truly learn? Who pays the price? And who has the power to stop it?
[serious] Because school isn't simply a building full of classrooms. It's a game. And until we understand the players...
[mysterious] ...we'll keep blaming the students for losing a game they never designed.
