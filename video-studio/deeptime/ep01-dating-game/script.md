## CHAPTER 1: FOR MOST OF HISTORY, YOU DIDN'T PLAY
*Visual: animated parchment map, Rome → medieval castle → imperial China → India; family elders shaking hands over a contract; coins, land and soldiers flow between two family crests.*

15. [curious] But first, a question. Who used to play this game?
16. For most of human history... it wasn't you.
17. In ancient Rome, in medieval Europe, in imperial China and across India, marriage was a deal between families.
18. It joined land. It joined money. It joined armies.
19. Love was a nice bonus. It was not the point.
20. Only in the last few centuries did a new idea spread: that you should choose your own partner, for love.
21. [serious] And the moment the choice moved from families to individuals... marriage became a market.
22. A market with competition, bargaining... and strategy.
23. And wherever there is strategy, there is game theory.

## CHAPTER 2: WHAT IS A GAME?
*Visual: 1944 book on a desk, young John Nash at a blackboard; four big animated icons (players, strategies, payoffs, information) lock into a board-game layout.*

24. Game theory is the mathematics of decisions that depend on other people's decisions.
25. It took off in nineteen forty-four, with a book by John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern.
26. A few years later, a young mathematician named John Nash gave it its most famous idea.
27. Every game has four parts.
28. Players. The people making choices.
29. Strategies. The moves each player can make.
30. Payoffs. What each player gets at the end.
31. And information. What each player knows... and what they don't.
32. Now look at dating through those four lenses.
33. The players: you, and everyone you might date.
34. The strategies: approach or wait. Commit, or keep your options open. Tell the truth... or show only your best side.
35. The payoffs: love, security, family, status... or heartbreak.
36. And the information? [emphasis] Terrible. You never really know what the other person is thinking.
37. That is exactly the kind of game that game theory was built for.

## CHAPTER 3: THE ALGORITHM OF LOVE
*Visual: chalkboard with 4 men and 4 women; animated proposal arrows, "MAYBE" tags, rejections falling like dominoes, final stable pairs light up gold.*

38. So let's go back to Gale and Shapley.
39. Imagine four men and four women. Each one secretly ranks everyone on the other side.
40. Here's their method. Step one: every man proposes to the woman at the top of his list.
41. Step two: each woman looks at her offers. She keeps the best one, but only as a "maybe." She rejects the rest.
42. Step three: every rejected man proposes to the next woman on his list.
43. And if a woman gets a better offer, she drops her "maybe" and trades up.
44. Round after round, the rejections fall like dominoes... until nobody is left out.
45. The result is what mathematicians call a stable match.
46. There is no man and woman who both prefer each other to the partners they ended up with. Nobody has a reason to run off.
47. [serious] Gale and Shapley proved this always works. Every time. For any number of people.
48. But here's the twist we promised.
49. The side that proposes gets the best partner it could have in any stable match.
50. The side that waits gets the worst.
51. And the math doesn't care who is a man and who is a woman. It only cares who moves first.
52. [curious] There's one more secret. The side that proposes can't gain anything by lying about what it wants.
53. But the side that waits sometimes can... by turning down offers it would actually accept.
54. In other words, there is real mathematics behind "playing hard to get."
55. And this isn't just theory. For decades, a version of this method has matched tens of thousands of new American doctors to hospitals every year.
56. It assigns students to high schools in New York City.
57. And in twenty twelve, it helped Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth win the Nobel Prize in Economics.

## CHAPTER 4: SIGNALS (WHY CHEAP WORDS DON'T WORK)
*Visual: two characters with thought bubbles hiding question marks; 1973 office, a diploma glowing; a peacock fans its tail while a fox watches; phone with endless "hey" messages vs. a car driving three hours.*

58. But real dating has a problem the algorithm ignores.
59. In the algorithm, everyone knows exactly what they want.
60. In real life, everyone is hiding information.
61. Is he serious, or just passing time? Is she interested, or just being polite?
62. Game theorists call this a signaling game.
63. In nineteen seventy-three, the economist Michael Spence asked why employers care so much about degrees.
64. His answer: a degree is hard to get. And a signal only works if it's costly to fake.
65. Nature figured this out long before economists did.
66. Look at the peacock. His giant tail is heavy, slow, and attracts predators.
67. That's exactly why it works. Only a truly healthy bird can afford to carry it.
68. Biologists call this the handicap principle.
69. Now think about dating. Anyone can type "I love you." It costs nothing.
70. But driving three hours to see someone? Remembering what they told you a month ago? Introducing them to your family?
71. Those are costly signals. And costly signals are the ones we believe.
72. [curious] Which is why the cheapest messages, the copy-paste compliments, the endless "hey"... mean almost nothing.

## CHAPTER 5: THE TRUST TRAP
*Visual: payoff matrix built as a 2×2 board with coins; the two characters each hold a "trust" card behind their back; Axelrod's 1980 tournament as tiny robots playing each other; "shadow of the future" as a long shadow cast forward on the chessboard.*

73. Even when two people like each other, there's another danger.
74. In nineteen fifty, researchers at the RAND Corporation came up with a game that still haunts economists. The prisoner's dilemma.
75. Two players. Each can cooperate... or betray.
76. If both cooperate, both win. If one betrays the other, the betrayer wins big, and the loyal one loses everything.
77. So each player thinks: "If I can't be sure of you... maybe I should protect myself."
78. And when both think that way... both lose.
79. In dating, cooperating means opening up. Investing. Committing.
80. Betraying means keeping one foot out the door. Keeping your options open, just in case.
81. [serious] When both people play it safe, the relationship never really starts.
82. So how do you escape the trap?
83. In nineteen eighty, the political scientist Robert Axelrod held a tournament. Computer programs played the prisoner's dilemma against each other, again and again.
84. The winner was one of the simplest strategies entered. It was called "tit for tat."
85. Start by cooperating. Then simply copy whatever the other player did last time.
86. Be nice. Respond quickly. Forgive quickly.
87. The lesson is powerful. Betrayal can pay in a single game. But in a game that repeats, trust wins.
88. Axelrod called it "the shadow of the future." When you expect to meet again tomorrow, you behave better today.
89. And a relationship... is the ultimate repeated game.

## CHAPTER 6: THE CASINO IN YOUR POCKET
*Visual: woman in bed with the glowing phone; thousands of profile cards fan out; a pyramid where a few profiles at the top glow with hearts; the slot machine returns; Skinner's lab with a pigeon pressing a lever.*

90. Which brings us to the dating app.
91. For the first time in history, you can browse thousands of possible partners from your bed.
92. That sounds like a dream. Game theory says it's a trap.
93. First, when options feel endless, the shadow of the future disappears. Why invest in one person when a new face is one swipe away?
94. Second, attention piles up at the top. On many apps, a small share of profiles gets a large share of the likes, while most people hear almost nothing.
95. And third... look at how it feels.
96. [curious] Swipe. Nothing. Swipe. Nothing. Swipe... a match!
97. In the nineteen fifties, the psychologist B. F. Skinner found that rewards which arrive at random keep animals pressing a lever longer than any other kind.
98. It's the same trick that powers slot machines.
99. So the app's game and your game are not the same game.
100. You want to find someone and leave. The app wins when you keep playing.

## CHAPTER 7: WHEN TO STOP SEARCHING
*Visual: a line of doors opening one by one; a progress bar fills to 37 percent in gold; after that, the next person who beats everyone so far gets a gold star.*

101. So how much searching is enough?
102. Mathematicians have an answer for that too. It's called the secretary problem, and it became famous in nineteen sixty.
103. Imagine you'll meet a series of people, one at a time. Once you say no to someone, you can't go back.
104. The best strategy? Spend the first thirty-seven percent just looking. Don't commit to anyone.
105. After that, choose the very next person who is better than everyone you've seen so far.
106. It doesn't guarantee the perfect partner. But no other strategy gives you a better chance of finding the very best one.
107. [curious] The real lesson isn't the number. It's this: searching forever is a losing strategy too.

## CHAPTER 8: HOW TO WIN THE DATING GAME
*Visual: four gold "lesson" cards flip onto the chessboard one by one; final shot pulls back from the two characters to the night city, then the world map, hearts glowing everywhere.*

108. So let's put it all together.
109. Lesson one, from Gale and Shapley: the side that makes the first move does better. Don't just wait.
110. Lesson two, from the peacock: words are cheap. Send signals that cost something, and trust the signals others pay for.
111. Lesson three, from Axelrod: start with trust, respond quickly, forgive quickly, and play the long game.
112. Lesson four, from the secretary problem: explore... but know when to stop.
113. [serious] For thousands of years, families played this game for us.
114. Now, for the first time, we play it ourselves. Millions of us, every single night.
115. The rules were never written down.
116. [mysterious] But now... you know a few of them.
