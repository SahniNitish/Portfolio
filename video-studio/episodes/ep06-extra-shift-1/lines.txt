This is Marcus.
In seven months, he'll have eighty thousand dollars sitting in secret bank accounts.
Money that was never his.
This is the story of the extra shift.
Part one. The grind.
Marcus wiped down the same counter he'd wiped a thousand times before.
The fryer hissed. The drive through beeped.
A teenager in a BMW pulled up, barely looking at Marcus as he handed over the order.
The kid's watch probably cost more than Marcus made in three months.
Eight dollars above minimum wage. Supervisor title. No benefits worth mentioning.
Every morning Marcus dragged himself into that fluorescent lit box, smelling of grease and disappointment.
He watched people outside through the window. Real people. People with destinations. People with futures.
He was thirty four years old, managing a crew of teenagers who quit without notice.
Mopping floors when the night shift called in sick.
Eating leftover fries for dinner, because grocery shopping felt pointless.
His apartment was a four hundred square foot box, with a radiator that only worked when it felt like it.
But bills don't care about your dreams.
Then one Tuesday morning, the district manager called.
Marcus, bad news. Dave quit.
Dave was the store manager. Apparently Dave had been secretly interviewing at a competing chain for four months.
He got offered a regional director role, and walked out giving exactly zero days notice.
He left a sticky note on the office door that said simply, Done. Good luck.
Marcus got the call at seven a.m.
We need someone to step in immediately. You're the most reliable person we have.
We're bumping your hourly by eight dollars, effective immediately. Full manager package kicks in after ninety days probation.
Eight dollars more per hour.
Marcus did the math in the parking lot before walking in.
Forty hours times eight dollars. Three hundred and twenty dollars extra per week.
Three hundred and twenty dollars times four weeks. Roughly twelve hundred and eighty dollars extra per month.
He stood there in the cold air, and felt something he hadn't felt in years.
Hope.
For four months, Marcus felt genuinely good.
He moved out of the box apartment, into a one bedroom with actual heating.
He bought a used couch that didn't smell like the previous owner.
He started a savings account, for the first time in his adult life.
But then the reality of the manager role settled in, like bad weather.
The stress was a different beast entirely.
Now he was the one getting called at two a.m. when someone didn't show.
Now he was responsible when the health inspector came.
Now he dealt with corporate emails demanding metrics, and labor percentages, and customer satisfaction scores.
Same cage. Slightly better food.
The extra money started feeling like hazard pay, rather than progress.
A thousand dollars extra per month sounds like freedom, until you realize freedom actually costs about four thousand more than you make.
It started as a mistake.
Marcus hired a guy named Derek in early November.
Derek showed up for orientation, filled out his paperwork halfway, and never came back.
Didn't call. Didn't text. Just vanished. It happens more than people realize in fast food.
Marcus was drowning in everything else that week.
Corporate audit prep. Two employees quit the same day. The ice machine broke.
Derek's payroll setup sat incomplete in the system.
Here is where Marcus made the first decision. Not the last. The first.
The payroll system at the chain worked like this.
New employees got entered into the scheduling software.
Hours got logged automatically, if the profile was active.
Payment went to whatever account was listed during setup.
Derek's setup was incomplete. No bank account linked yet.
And Marcus, as manager, had system access to complete the onboarding.
Marcus told himself he was just going to fix it later.
Then he looked at the numbers.
Derek was showing forty hours logged for the week in the system.
Ghost hours.
Nobody caught it. Because Marcus approved the timesheets, and Marcus was the one corporate checked with.
Fifteen dollars per hour, times forty hours. Six hundred dollars per week.
Marcus sat in that small back office at eleven p.m. Tired. Smelling like a deep fryer.
Thinking about the BMW kid from the drive through.
He rerouted Derek's direct deposit, to a secondary account he opened at a different bank.
He told himself, just one week.
